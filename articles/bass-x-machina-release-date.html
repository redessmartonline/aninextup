Brian Tyree Henry’s New Netflix Anime: Bass X Machina Explained

What is Brian Tyree Henry’s new Netflix anime?

The title is Bass X Machina, a six-episode adult animated series that arrived on Netflix on November 3, 2026. Henry voices Bass Reeves and also serves as an executive producer. LeSean Thomas created the show, with animation by Studio Mir. It combines a Western setting with machinery and supernatural threats rather than presenting a conventional historical drama.

That combination explains why the series can appear under several descriptions: anime, adult animation, science fiction and Western. They point to different parts of the same project. For someone discovering it through Henry's name, the useful starting point is the character at the center: a father whose efforts to keep his family safe threaten to make him someone they can no longer trust.

The series is already released, so older articles calling it an upcoming show are describing its promotional period. Our original release guide has been updated here to cover the launch and Henry's role. The November 3 date is the Netflix premiere, not the date of a new casting announcement or the start of a separate television broadcast.

Who does Henry play, and what does his producer credit mean?

Netflix's Tudum introduction identifies Henry's Bass as a former soldier with a cybernetic arm. It also names Janelle Monáe as his wife, Glory Reeves, and Tati Gabrielle as aspiring engineer Dana Starr. These relationships make the premise more specific than a lone gunslinger moving from one opponent to the next: Bass has people to return to and people alongside him.

Henry's executive producer credit is separate from his voice performance. Netflix lists him with LeSean Thomas, Jennifer Wiley-Moxley and Chad Handley. That establishes an involvement beyond acting, but the credit alone does not document which individual story, casting or animation decisions he made. Thomas remains the credited creator, and Studio Mir the animation studio.

Our interest in the casting is how the role brings two kinds of authority together. Bass can be physically intimidating in the frontier setting while remaining vulnerable to what his family thinks of him. That is an interpretation of the announced setup, not an assessment of Henry's complete performance. A celebrity name may bring viewers to the title, but the character's conflicting responsibilities give them a clearer reason to investigate the story.

What is Bass X Machina about?

Netflix places the story in a steampunk West where outlaws, machines and supernatural forces endanger a father and his family. The official premise centers on protection becoming morally dangerous: the actions Bass takes in their defense could cost him the relationships he wants to preserve. His power creates a problem as well as a possible solution.

The opening episode's Netflix description gives a practical entry point. The US Army approaches bounty hunter Bass Reeves at Fort Hope for a mission involving a missing scientist and the threat of another civil war. That is enough to explain the initial assignment without reproducing later episode summaries or exposing where the conflict ends.

The premise is worth considering through the distance between an immediate victory and a lasting consequence. Defeating a threat can be straightforward within an action scene; returning to ordinary relationships after using that violence is a different challenge. The machines enlarge what Bass can do, while the family premise asks what those abilities mean to the people around him. Those two scales give a spoiler-free way to approach the show.

Is Bass Reeves a real person?

Yes. The historical Bass Reeves was born enslaved and later became a deputy US marshal. The National Park Service's Fort Smith bulletin records his move into federal law enforcement in 1875, serving the Western District of Arkansas and Indian Territory. This history is separate from the fictional show's cybernetic weapons and supernatural dangers.

The US Marshals Service describes Reeves as among the first known African American deputy US marshals west of the Mississippi. That wording is more precise than calling him the first Black marshal in American history. Readers interested in his actual career can start with those institutional histories rather than treating the animated series as a factual biography.

For the adaptation, the distinction makes room for a more useful question than whether every machine is historically accurate. What happens when a Black lawman's name becomes the center of a speculative frontier story? It puts him in the position of the figure shaping the adventure, rather than leaving that history as background. The imaginative changes should remain visible to viewers; enjoying the fantasy and learning the documented history are compatible activities.

Why is it described as anime?

Netflix's Annecy announcement groups Bass X Machina with internationally produced animation influenced by anime's storytelling and visual language. Its public title listing identifies English as the original audio. The announcement credits Studio Mir, so describing it simply as a Japanese television production would obscure how Netflix actually presents it.

For readers, those production details are more helpful than trying to settle every definition of anime in a release guide. An English original track, a streaming premiere and a credited animation studio tell you what to look for when you open the title. They also keep the show distinct from manga adaptations that happen to share Netflix's wider animation slate.

The steampunk setting provides another useful distinction. A mechanical body in a Western landscape suggests an encounter between personal force and larger systems of power. That observation follows from the premise and promotional imagery; it does not establish the creators' intentions in every sequence. It gives a viewer something concrete to notice without turning a genre label into a promise about quality.

How long is it, and where can you watch?

Watch through the official Netflix title page. Its current listing contains six episodes, with displayed lengths of 40, 29, 35, 33, 35 and 35 minutes. Together those rounded listings amount to about three hours and 27 minutes. The first episode is the longest, giving you a 40-minute starting commitment.

That compact season can suit viewers who want a contained evening or weekend watch, but six listed episodes do not by themselves prove that every storyline resolves. Nor do they announce a second season. We are describing the available season's length, without making a renewal claim or presenting the episode count as a guarantee of narrative closure.

The US page carries a TV-MA rating. Audio, subtitles and ratings should be checked in your own Netflix account, since the public page you encounter may reflect a different region. Henry's original English performance is the relevant track for readers arriving through his name. Select the audio you want before comparing a dubbed performance with the credited original cast.

What to know before pressing play

Start with the expectation of an adult speculative Western led by Henry, rather than a straightforward account of Reeves's career. The attraction of the setup is its competing demands: a father wants to protect his family, a bounty hunter is asked to undertake a dangerous job, and a technologically altered body makes force available on a different scale.

If the visual style catches your attention, watch the first episode before searching for ending explanations. Netflix's episode descriptions become more revealing as the list continues.
